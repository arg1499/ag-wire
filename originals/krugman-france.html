Krugman: France may be 'too big to save'

A Nobel economist warns of an 'ugly crisis.' A former ECB chief begs politicians to compromise. The eurozone's fault line is showing.

October 10, 2026 · 9:10 AM ET

A Nobel economist warns of an 'ugly crisis.' A former ECB chief begs politicians to compromise. The eurozone's fault line is showing.

Paul Krugman doesn't do subtle. The Nobel economist is now warning that France may have become "too big to save," sounding the alarm on a looming "ugly crisis." Jean-Claude Trichet — the former ECB president, not a man given to panic — says French politicians must compromise.

Read those two sentences together and feel the temperature drop. When the economists start using words like "ugly" and "too big to save" about the eurozone's second-largest economy, they're not describing a policy disagreement. They're describing a doom loop: political paralysis meets unsustainable borrowing meets a central bank with limited room to maneuver.

Here's the part that should worry investors more than the headlines: France isn't Greece. You can't quarantine it with a bailout fund and a stern lecture. It's too large to rescue and too interconnected to fail cleanly. "Too big to save" is the phrase that ends the conversation about European backstops — because if the backstop can't hold France, the backstop is decorative.

Why it matters: Every diversified portfolio has eurozone exposure, whether it knows it or not. A French fiscal crisis doesn't stay French — it reprices European sovereign risk, hits the euro, and forces the ECB into choices it doesn't want to make. Trichet begging politicians to compromise is the tell: the economists have run the numbers, and the numbers need politics to cooperate. They rarely do.
